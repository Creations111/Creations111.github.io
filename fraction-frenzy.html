My role: CAD Lead and Project Manager

I designed the drivetrain for both belts: the dowels, the stands, and the shaft fits. I made the key SolidWorks parts and the full assembly, went through 7 CAD iterations, printed the conveyor parts and game pieces, and set up the belts. I also ran the schedule for our 5-person team.

01Problem and requirements

Our client was a 5th-grade class at Melrose Leadership Academy in Oakland, California, practicing adding fractions with unlike denominators. They wanted something team-based, competitive, and hands-on.

RequirementTarget
Piece alignmentThe belt stops each piece in line with the board squares
Game length5 minutes or less, so the class can rotate through many players
MovementA correct answer moves the team’s piece a random 1–3 steps
InputBoth teams can type answers at the same time
Envelope14″ × 14″ × 4″ tabletop board

03Analysis

Why a servo couldn’t drive the belt

s = r × θ

Our first plan used servos. A servo only sweeps 180° (π rad), so on our earlier, smaller dowel it moved the belt about 4 cm, then had to swing back and drag the pieces with it. A DC motor keeps turning, so it can move a piece as far as it needs to and bring every piece back at the end.

Meeting the 5-minute limit

12 steps ÷ (1 to 3 per answer)

At one step per answer, every game took 12 correct answers, which was too slow for 5 minutes. We added an LED spinner with 8 sections worth 1–3 steps, so a team needs 4 to 12 correct answers instead of always 12.

Pin budget

≈32 pins needed > 26 GPIO

Our first wiring plan needed about 32 pins, and the Pico only has 26. Running both belts off one motor driver and putting the keypads and LCDs on I2C adapters got us under.

04Design

Shaft-to-stand fit

Each dowel shaft is Ø0.57″ and runs in a Ø0.60″ bore in the stand, a designed diametral clearance of 0.03″ (0.76 mm), about 0.38 mm per side. With too little clearance, the printed dowels jammed from friction. With too much, they wobbled and the belt drifted.

Keeping the belt on track

I added flanged lips at both ends of each dowel to keep the belt from walking off sideways, and wrapped the dowels in sandpaper for traction. The shaft ends have a hex socket for the motor connection (Detail B on the dowel drawing in the package below).

Integrating the full assembly

I put everyone’s parts into one SolidWorks assembly with section and exploded views, so we could check how the stands, belts, ratchet frame, and enclosure fit together before printing or cutting anything.

05Build, test, revise

About ten weeks from a cardboard mockup to a working board. These are the notes from each stage.

  1. 0

    Feb 10–12

    Cardboard mockup

    Test
    Full-size cardboard board with sandpaper-wrapped cardboard rollers and a stitched cloth belt.
    Problem
    The layout worked, but cardboard couldn’t keep the rollers lined up.
    Change
    Model the rollers and stands in SolidWorks and 3D-print them.
  2. 1

    Feb 19–25

    First 3D-printed axles

    Test
    I modeled the axle and dowel in SolidWorks and printed them to test material, dimensions, and the fit to a DC gear motor.
    Problem
    In version 1, nothing held the motor in line with the shaft.
    Change
    Version 2 seated the motor in a printed holder so the shaft stayed aligned under load.
  3. 2

    Feb 25

    First powered belt

    Test
    Printed rollers, motor mount, and electronics all running together in the cardboard box.
    Result
    First time a motor moved a game piece on the belt. We kept testing on this setup after that.
  4. 3

    Mar 10–21

    Fixing drift and friction in CAD

    Test
    I measured the belt’s sideways drift with a ruler on the printed conveyor.
    Problem
    The belt drifted a lot, and parts bound up while it ran.
    Root cause
    Dimensional errors in the 3D-printed dowels and stands. The first stands were too thick, the motor-holder stand sat at the wrong height, and one part rubbed against the belt.
    Change
    I revised the CAD: slimmed the stands, corrected the motor-mount height, set the 0.03″ shaft clearance, added flanged lips to the dowels, wrapped them in sandpaper, and redesigned the part that was rubbing.
    Result
    After 7 CAD iterations the motor mounts were steady, and drift was down 5 mm when I measured it again.
  5. 4

    Mar 17–20

    Plywood enclosure and rubber belts

    Change
    The team moved everything into the laser-cut 14″ × 14″ × 4″ plywood enclosure and replaced the cloth belts with rubber. I printed the conveyor parts.
    Problem
    Several glues failed to bond the Velcro (which holds the game pieces) to the rubber.
    Result
    We sewed the Velcro on instead. Machine sewing was cleaner, stronger, and faster than hand stitching, so we machine-sewed the second belt.
  6. 5

    Apr 2–9 · Days before the showcase

    The conveyors stopped working

    Problem
    The conveyors stopped moving even though nothing in the mechanism had changed.
    First fix
    Sanding and lubricant got them moving again, but they stayed inconsistent.
    Root cause
    The ratchet frame was pushing the stands into the dowels, adding friction the motors couldn’t overcome.
    Change
    We cut the frame in half and added spacing between the stands and the dowels.
    Result
    Both conveyors ran for the showcase.
  7. 6

    Apr 13

    Mid-span belt sag

    Problem
    One belt sagged in the middle and struggled to stay straight.
    Root cause
    Not enough tension mid-span, and the stands couldn’t move any farther back on the ratchet to add more.
    Change
    We added a spare dowel and stand in the middle as a support roller.
    Result
    The sag went down.
  8. ✓

    April 2026

    Final build

    We painted the board in a meadow theme and made instruction brochures in English and Spanish.

07Lessons and next revision

  • Design belt guides from the start. Guide blocks, or more precise stand placement, would have prevented much of the drift we spent weeks fixing.
  • Add features earlier. We added the spinner near the end, so we had to redo wiring and code right before the deadline.
  • Learn the other parts of the project. If I had known MicroPython and wiring better, I could have helped more when those parts needed fixing at the end.
  • Work in small steps. Prints take hours, so a little progress every day worked much better for us than catching up at the end.
