My role: CAD Lead and Project Manager

I modeled the key SolidWorks parts and the full assembly, designed the drivetrain for both belts (dowels, stands, and shaft fits), revised the CAD through 7 iterations to cut drift, 3D-printed the conveyor parts and game pieces, wrapped the dowels and tensioned the belts, coded the victory tunes, and coordinated our 5-person team to an on-time build.

01Problem and requirements

Fifth graders at Melrose Leadership Academy in Oakland, California were struggling to add fractions with unlike denominators. The client asked for something team-based, competitive, and hands-on. We turned that into measurable requirements before designing anything.

RequirementTarget
Piece alignmentThe belt stops each piece in line with the board squares
Game length5 minutes or less, so the class can rotate through many players
MovementA correct answer moves the team’s piece a random 1–3 steps
InputBoth teams can type answers at the same time
Envelope14″ × 14″ × 4″ tabletop board

02Concept selection

The team scored 12 concepts in a weighted decision matrix: teaches math 30%, team-based 20%, ease of construction 15%, competitive 10%, engaging 10%, hands-on 10%, and environmentally friendly 5%. Robot Race scored highest, with the board game close behind. We chose the board game because, within our timeline, our skills were best suited to delivering a fun, fully working game with game pieces the kids could choose.

03Analysis

Why a servo couldn’t drive the belt

s = r × θ

Belt travel s equals the dowel radius r times the angle turned θ. A servo’s full sweep is 180° (π rad), so on our earlier, smaller dowel it moved the belt only about 4 cm before it had to swing back, pulling the belt and pieces back with it. A continuous DC motor drives the belt forward as far as the game needs, then runs every piece back to start at the end.

Meeting the 5-minute limit

12 steps ÷ (1 to 3 per answer)

At one step per correct answer, every game needed 12 rounds, which was longer than the 5-minute target. The team added an 8-section LED spinner valued 1–3, so a team now needs between 4 and 12 correct answers to finish instead of always 12.

Pin budget

≈32 pins needed > 26 GPIO

The first wiring plan needed about 32 GPIO pins, more than the Raspberry Pi Pico’s 26. Running both belts from a single motor driver and moving the keypads and LCDs onto I2C adapters brought the design within budget.

04Design

Shaft-to-stand fit

Each dowel shaft is Ø0.57″ and runs in a Ø0.60″ bore in the stand, a designed diametral clearance of 0.03″ (0.76 mm), about 0.38 mm per side. With too little clearance, the printed dowels jammed from friction. With too much, they wobbled and the belt drifted.

Keeping the belt on track

I added flanged lips at both ends of each dowel to keep the belt from walking off sideways, and wrapped the dowels in sandpaper for traction. The shaft ends carry a hex socket for a keyed drive connection (Detail B on the dowel drawing).

Integrating the full assembly

I combined every team member’s parts into one SolidWorks assembly with section and exploded views. That let us check fits between the stands, belts, ratchet frame, and enclosure before printing or cutting anything.

05Build, test, revise

From a cardboard mockup to a laser-cut, motor-driven board in about ten weeks. Each stage records what we tested, what went wrong, why, and what changed.

  1. 0

    Feb 10–12

    Cardboard mockup

    Test
    A full-size cardboard layout with sandpaper-wrapped cardboard rollers on cardboard stands and a stitched cloth belt.
    Problem
    The board layout worked, but the cardboard couldn’t hold the rollers in alignment.
    Change
    Move the rollers and stands to 3D-printed parts modeled in SolidWorks.
  2. 1

    Feb 19–25

    First 3D-printed axles

    Test
    I modeled the axle and dowel in SolidWorks and printed them to test material, dimensions, and the fit to a DC gear motor.
    Problem
    In version 1, nothing held the motor in line with the shaft.
    Change
    Version 2 seated the motor in a printed holder so the shaft stayed aligned under load.

    Decision: continuous DC motors, not servos

    Our first plan drove each belt with a servo. Because a servo only swings 180° back and forth, its belt travel is limited by the dowel radius: about 4 cm on our earlier dowel, and the belt would be pulled back on every return. We switched to continuous DC gear motors on one driver, which move the pieces forward as far as needed and return them all at the end.

  3. 2

    Feb 25

    First powered belt

    Test
    The printed rollers, motor mount, and electronics running together in the cardboard box.
    Result
    For the first time, a belt carried a game piece under motor power. This became the test rig for everything that followed.
  4. 3

    Mar 10–21

    Fixing drift and friction in CAD

    Test
    I measured the belt’s sideways drift with a ruler on the printed conveyor.
    Problem
    Drift started out large, and parts bound up as the belt ran.
    Root cause
    Dimensional errors in the 3D-printed dowels and stands. The first stands were too thick, the motor-holder stand sat at the wrong height, and one part rubbed against the belt.
    Change
    I revised the CAD: slimmed the stands, corrected the motor-mount height, set the 0.03″ shaft clearance, added flanged lips to the dowels, wrapped them in sandpaper, and redesigned the part that was rubbing.
    Result
    Across 7 CAD iterations, the motor mounts stabilized and drift dropped by 5 mm when re-measured with a ruler.
  5. 4

    Mar 17–20

    Plywood enclosure and rubber belts

    Change
    The team moved everything into the laser-cut 14″ × 14″ × 4″ plywood enclosure and replaced the cloth belts with rubber. I printed the conveyor parts.
    Problem
    Several glues failed to bond the Velcro (which holds the game pieces) to the rubber.
    Result
    The Velcro was sewn on instead. Machine sewing proved cleaner, more durable, and faster than hand stitching, so the second belt was machine sewn.
  6. 5

    Apr 2–9 · Days before the showcase

    The conveyors stopped working

    Problem
    The conveyors stopped moving even though nothing in the mechanism had changed.
    First fix
    Sanding and lubricant got them moving again, but they stayed inconsistent.
    Root cause
    The ratchet frame was pushing the stands into the dowels, adding friction the motors couldn’t overcome.
    Change
    The team cut the frame in half and added spacing between the stands and the dowels.
    Result
    The issue was resolved, and both conveyors ran for the showcase.
  7. 6

    Apr 13

    Mid-span belt sag

    Problem
    One belt sagged in the middle and struggled to stay straight.
    Root cause
    Not enough tension mid-span, and the stands couldn’t move any farther back on the ratchet to add more.
    Change
    The team added a spare dowel and stand in the middle as a support roller.
    Result
    Testing showed it reduced the sag.
  8. ✓

    April 2026

    Final build, tested with students

    We painted the board in a meadow theme and printed bilingual instruction brochures in English and Spanish. The game was delivered on deadline and presented at Melrose Leadership Academy.

07Lessons and next revision

  • Design belt guides from the start. Guide blocks, or more precise stand placement, would have prevented much of the drift we spent weeks fixing.
  • Integrate late features early. Adding the spinner near the end forced wiring and code rework under deadline pressure.
  • Cross-train the team. If I had gone deeper into MicroPython and clean wiring, we would have struggled less at the end. I’m building that broader skill set now.
  • Work incrementally. Steady daily progress beats late sprints, especially when parts take hours to print.

My role: Undergraduate Research Assistant

I design and fabricate the hardware that holds and magnetizes our samples: stands, sample holders, and custom electromagnets. I also run magnetostriction measurements and process FMR data alongside graduate researchers.

01Problem and requirements

Our lab measures how sputtered magnetic thin films respond to magnetic fields along different axes. Each sample sits on a PCB that has to be held at a precise, repeatable angle between a magnet’s poles, and some samples couldn’t be tested in the lab’s industrial magnets at all.

RequirementWhy it matters
Repeatable sample angleEasy- and hard-axis measurements are only comparable if every sample returns to the same orientation
Quick PCB swapsMany samples are measured in one session
Locks to the rotation stageThe holder and stage must move as one piece, with no play
Rigid and non-magneticFlex shifts the sample, and magnetic material would distort the field being measured
At least 200 G at the sampleThe minimum field needed for samples tested outside the industrial magnets

02Build, test, revise

  1. 1

    3D-printed stands

    Design
    My first stands for the measurement setup were 3D printed, which made them fast to make and iterate.
    Problem
    Printed plastic is far less stiff than metal, and stiffness matters when a sample has to stay exactly in place.
    Change
    Redesign the stands to be waterjet cut from aluminum.
  2. 2

    Waterjet-cut aluminum stands

    Design
    I redesigned the stands to be waterjet cut from aluminum.
    Why aluminum
    It’s non-magnetic, so it won’t distort the field around the sample, and it’s much stiffer than printed plastic.
  3. 3

    Indexed sample holder

    Design
    A 3D-printed holder accepts the sample PCB with about 0.3 mm of clearance, so it slides in and out easily without play.
    Interface
    Features on the bottom of the holder drop into the rotation stage’s holes, locking the holder and stage together as one piece so every sample returns to the same orientation.
  4. 4

    Custom electromagnets

    Need
    Some samples couldn’t be tested in the lab’s industrial magnets, but still needed at least 200 G.
    Design
    I wound my own electromagnets with 250 turns on each coil. To get a stronger field, I designed for more turns, a better core material, and a smaller pole gap.
    Test
    I measured the field with a gaussmeter probe.
    Result
    Up to 800 G (about 800 Oe in air) at under 5 A, four times the required field. That margin lets us run at lower current, which means less heating and safer operation.
  5. →

    In progress

    Stand for a motorized rotation stage

    I’m now designing a stand for the lab’s electrically powered rotation stage.

01Problem and requirements

Most cars have front and rear proximity sensors, but the sides are largely unprotected. A cyclist, or a child on a bike, alongside a turning car sits right in that blind zone. I prototyped a scaled-down side sensor that warns the driver more urgently as an object gets closer.

RequirementTarget
DetectionObjects on both sides of the vehicle, whether they move or not
AlertBuzzer and LED speed up as an object gets closer
False alarmsNo alert beyond the threshold distance
Packaging and powerCar-shaped enclosure, powered over USB-C
CostLow-cost student build

02Concept selection

I compared sensing options with a Kepner-Tregoe decision analysis. The deciding factor was physics: what each sensor can actually detect.

OptionHow it sensesLimitationDecision
PIR motion sensorPassively detects changes in infrared (heat)Blind to stationary, non-heat-emitting objects such as curbsScrapped
Ultrasonic sensor (HC-SR04) on a servoTimes the echo of a sound pulse to measure distanceNarrow beam, solved by sweeping it on a servoSelected

03Analysis

Echo timing

d = v × t ÷ 2

With sound at 343 m/s, an object 50 cm away returns its echo in about 2.9 ms, and one at 2 cm in about 0.12 ms. The 2 cm lower limit is roughly the HC-SR04’s minimum sensing distance.

Why each sensor sweeps

w = 2 × d × tan(15° ÷ 2)

An HC-SR04 sees a cone only about 15° wide, roughly 13 cm across at 50 cm. A fixed sensor would miss most of the side of the car, so each sensor sweeps 180° back and forth on a servo to cover the whole side.

04Design

Graded alertsThe Pico maps distance to alert rate, so the buzzer beeps faster and the LED flashes faster as an object closes in. Nothing triggers beyond the threshold.
Friction-fit componentsI sized the buzzer and LED holes slightly under their diameters, so the laser-cut plywood grips them without fasteners.
Servo seating and powerRectangular openings in the top panel let the servos seat and rotate freely, and a side opening gives access to the USB-C port.

05Build, test, revise

  1. 1

    Nov 9, 2025 · Scrapped

    Motion-sensor prototype

    Test
    A PIR motion sensor and a buzzer in a cardboard box.
    Problem
    It worked on the bench, but stayed silent next to stationary obstacles.
    Root cause
    A motion sensor only reacts to things that move or give off heat. A curb does neither.
    Change
    Switch to ultrasonic distance sensing, chosen with a Kepner-Tregoe analysis.
  2. 2

    Nov 20, 2025

    Ultrasonic redesign

    Test
    I simulated the new circuit, then breadboarded two HC-SR04 sensors on 3D-printed servo mounts.
    Result
    Both sensors swept 180° and reported distance to stationary and moving objects.
  3. 3

    Nov 20 – Dec 3, 2025

    Enclosure revisions

    Test
    A finger-jointed plywood enclosure designed in AutoCAD and laser-cut at the Northeastern Makerspace.
    Problem
    The component holes and servo openings came out the wrong size.
    Change
    I recut the panels over several iterations until the friction fits and servo openings were right.
    Lesson
    Cut a small test piece first instead of the whole enclosure, which saves time and material.
  4. ✓

    Dec 3, 2025 · Final showcase

    Fully functional, on deadline

    Both sensors sweep and alert at the same time across the 2–50 cm range. Going from the scrapped concept to this demo took 3 weeks.
