Fraction Frenzy: a conveyor-belt board game for 5th-grade math

My role: CAD Lead and Project Manager

I modeled all of the key SolidWorks parts and the final assembly, designed the drivetrain for both conveyor belts, 3D-printed the dowels and game pieces, tensioned the belts, supported the wiring and MicroPython, and coordinated our 5-person team to an on-time build.

Watch demo View code

The problem

Fifth graders at Melrose Leadership Academy in Oakland, California were struggling with adding fractions that have unlike denominators. The client asked for something team-based, competitive, and hands-on that would make that practice fun.

How it works

A fraction problem appears on both LCDs, and each team types its answer on a keypad. A correct answer triggers an 8-section LED spinner valued 1–3, and that team’s conveyor belt carries its game piece forward. The first team to step 12 wins, and both belts reset.

Design iterations

From a cardboard mockup to a laser-cut, motor-driven board in about ten weeks. Click any photo to enlarge it.

  1. 0

    Feb 10–12

    Cardboard mockup

    We built the first full-size layout from cardboard: a box with two belt channels and LCD frames, sandpaper-wrapped cardboard rollers on cardboard stands, and a stitched canvas belt. It proved the board layout worked, but the cardboard couldn’t hold the rollers in alignment.

    Board mockup Cardboard roller Canvas belt
  2. 1

    Feb 23–25

    First 3D-printed axles

    I modeled the axle in SolidWorks and printed it to test material, dimensions, and how well it coupled to a DC gear motor. The second version seated the motor in a printed holder so the shaft stayed aligned under load.

    Axle v1 Axle v2 with motor holder

    Design decision: DC gear motors, not servos

    Our first plan drove each belt with a servo. A servo only sweeps 180°, and our calculation showed that would move the belt about 4 cm per actuation, which isn’t enough to cross the board. Continuous DC gear motors on a single motor driver could run each belt independently for as far as the game needed.

  3. 2

    Feb 25

    First powered belt

    The printed rollers, motor mount, and electronics ran together in the cardboard box. This was the first time a belt carried a game piece under motor power, and it gave us a test rig for everything that followed.

    First working conveyor
  4. 3

    Mar 17–21

    CAD revisions to stabilize the mounts and stop drift

    Testing exposed several problems. The first printed stands were too thick and wasted space, the motor-holder stand sat at the wrong height, and one part created excess friction on the belt. I slimmed the stands, corrected the motor mount, and revised the friction part. I also gave the dowels side ramps and sandpaper wraps so the belt tracked straight. Across 7 CAD iterations, these changes stabilized the motor mounts and cut belt drift by 5 mm.

    Revised stand Stand + motor holder Revised dowel
    Assembly drawing with section and exploded views
  5. 4

    Mar 19

    Plywood enclosure and rubber belts

    The team moved everything into the laser-cut 14″ × 14″ × 4″ plywood enclosure. We also swapped the canvas belts for rubber, with Velcro sewn on to hold the game pieces.

    First full plywood build
  6. ✓

    April 2026

    Final build, tested with students

    We added the LED spinner, painted the board in a meadow theme, and printed bilingual instruction brochures in English and Spanish. The game was delivered on deadline and presented at Melrose Leadership Academy, where 15 users gave it an average rating of 8/10 across all criteria.

Vehicle side-proximity sensor

The problem

Most cars have front and rear proximity sensors, but the sides are largely unprotected. A cyclist or a child on a bike alongside a turning car sits right in that blind zone. I set out to prototype a scaled-down side sensor that warns the driver more urgently as an object gets closer.

How it works

A Raspberry Pi Pico running MicroPython sweeps two servo-mounted ultrasonic sensors across each side. As an object closes in from 50 cm to 2 cm, the buzzer beeps faster and the LED flashes faster. Anything beyond the threshold triggers nothing, which prevents false alarms.

Watch final demo View code

Design iterations

One scrapped concept, one redesign, and several enclosure revisions between November 9 and December 3.

  1. 1

    Nov 9, 2025 · Scrapped

    Motion-sensor prototype

    My first version used a PIR motion sensor and a buzzer in a cardboard box. It worked on the bench, but it had a fundamental flaw: a motion sensor only triggers on things that move or give off heat. A curb doesn’t move, so the system would stay silent beside exactly the kind of obstacle a driver needs to know about. I scrapped it at the first check-in.

    V1 cardboard prototype V1 circuit

    Design decision: measure distance, not motion

    I compared the alternatives with a Kepner-Tregoe decision analysis and chose ultrasonic distance sensors. They report how far away an object is whether or not it’s moving. Mounting them on servos lets each sensor sweep the full side field of view instead of a single fixed line.

  2. 2

    Nov 20, 2025

    Ultrasonic redesign

    I simulated the new circuit first, then breadboarded two HC-SR04 sensors on 3D-printed servo mounts. The logic maps distance bands to alert rates, so the buzzer and LED speed up as an object closes in.

    V2 circuit Breadboard build Control logic
  3. 3

    Nov 20 – Dec 3, 2025

    Enclosure revisions

    I designed a finger-jointed plywood enclosure in AutoCAD and laser-cut it at the Northeastern Makerspace. It took several cuts to get the openings right. I sized the buzzer and LED holes slightly under their diameters for a friction fit, cut rectangular openings so the servos could seat and rotate freely, and added a side port for USB power.

    AutoCAD cut file Inside the enclosure
  4. ✓

    Dec 3, 2025 · Final showcase

    Fully functional, on deadline

    Both sensors run at the same time, and the alerts ramp up across the 2–50 cm range. Going from the scrapped motion-sensor concept to this demo took 3 weeks. Next time, I’d replace the tape on the sensor mounts with 3D-printed brackets and route the wiring more cleanly.
